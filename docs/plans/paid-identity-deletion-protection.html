§1 — A provenance edit removed 93 identities in eight seconds, and $43 of spend had produced them

On 2026-09-08 a repair pass corrected a false origin value on 2,096 StandardName identities — residue from an export and import round trip that no longer exists in the code. It moved 1,179 to pipeline, 246 to derived and 671 to null. Between 99 and 107 seconds later, a structural cleanup pass deleted 78 of the identities the repair had just reclassified, and 93 in total.

Nothing malfunctioned. Every component did what it was written to do. The loss came from a coupling that no comment, test, or guard records:

The pieces, each verifiable in the source:

  • _query_derived_parents_for_admission_cleanup selects WHERE parent.origin = 'derived' AND parent.name_stage IN ['pending','accepted']. Its own docstring states the restriction exists “so that catalog-authoritative and pipeline-authored names are never touched by this cleanup” — so origin is the safety boundary, written as a comment rather than as a guard.
  • is_admissible_parent_name decides whether a name “deserves a StandardName placeholder + HAS_PARENT edge”. That is a question about scaffolding, not about whether a physics quantity is legitimate.
  • normalize_derived_parent_lifecycle passes every inadmissible id straight to _delete_derived_parent_nodes with no volume ceiling, no dry run, no publication check, and no call to filter_protected — which exists in protection.py for exactly this purpose and is never invoked here.
  • The change row a deletion writes carries from_name, to_name, operation, origin, reason and changed_at and nothing else. So the record proves a deletion happened and cannot support undoing it.

The asymmetry this exposes is the finding. Every control in this codebase sits on the write path for names: claim tokens, compare-and-set preconditions, quorum gates, filter_protected, approval as the sole writer of active. There is no equivalent control on the delete path, and no invariant anywhere states that a published identity is undeletable. etendue_of_spectrometer_channel carried three unchanged_ratification records and sat in a published review cut of 208 names; none of that was visible to the query that condemned it.

§2 — Recorded spend is the boundary, and it is a better instrument than provenance

An identity on which money has been spent must not be deletable by an automatic pass. Not gated, not warned about — refused. A human or an explicitly authorised operation may still remove it; a cleanup sweep may not.

This is not a proxy for the real rule, it is the real rule. Spend is the durable evidence that a person or a pipeline invested in an identity: a name was composed, reviewers were paid to judge it, documentation was generated and refined. That investment is exactly what a structural sweep has no standing to discard.

The metric was validated against the loss before this plan was written, and it outperforms the explanation the incident first suggested:

TestProtectsMisses
“was it reclassified by the origin repair?” 78 of 93 15, including the published cut name etendue_of_spectrometer_channel
“did it cost money?” 75 of 93 18, all with zero recorded spend

Three properties make it the right choice. It is independent of how an identity got labelled, so it cannot be switched off by a provenance edit — which is precisely how this loss happened. It catches nine identities the provenance reasoning misses entirely, among them the one name from the published cut. And it discriminates: the 18 zero-spend rows are plausibly genuine free scaffolding, so the cleanup keeps its legitimate job rather than being disabled.

One caveat to carry into implementation: standard_name_ids is a list property on LLMCost and there is no index on it, so a naive any(x IN c.standard_name_ids WHERE x IN $names) is a scan of roughly 37,000 rows. It completed inside the ten-second login-node ceiling at that size, but a per-identity call inside a delete loop would not. The guard needs a set-based pre-check or a materialised PAID_FOR edge, decided in §3.

§3 — The guard: give the delete path the protections the write path already has

One node. It restores nothing — it makes restoration safe by ensuring a restored identity cannot be destroyed again by the next pipeline run.

Done when no identity carrying recorded spend, a promotion record, a catalog binding, or name_stage = 'approved' can be removed by an automatic pass, whatever its origin says; and a pass whose candidate set exceeds a stated ceiling refuses in full and reports rather than executing part of it.

Four requirements, in priority order:

  1. Spend is a hard refusal. Any identity with a non-zero LLMCost attribution is undeletable by an automatic path. The refusal must name the identity, its spend, and the protection that held it — a silent skip is as bad as a silent delete. Decide between a set-based pre-check and a materialised edge, and state the measured cost of the choice.
  2. Publication state is a hard refusal too, independent of spend: an identity any catalog cut names, one carrying an unchanged_ratification or other promotion record, or one at name_stage = 'approved'. Route through filter_protected if that function can express it — but read its definition first. It matches name_stage = 'approved', and zero rows currently hold that value, so it returns the empty set for every input it is given today. A function named for catalog protection that protects nothing is worse than no function.
  3. A volume ceiling that refuses the whole pass. Ninety-three identities in eight seconds is currently indistinguishable from correct operation. Choose the ceiling from the measured history — this path's per-day totals this month are 104, 180, 132, 125, 74, 327, 116 and 155 — and justify the number. Partial execution is not acceptable: a pass that would breach the ceiling reports its candidate set and stops.
  4. A deletion must carry its own recovery material. Write the removed node's properties and its edge inventory into the change row or an adjacent tombstone, so the graph can answer what was lost without reaching for a store dump.

The scope is the class, not the function. There are 37 DETACH DELETE statements across graph_ops.py and provenance_lifecycle.py, and nine call sites of the deletion_change_cypher helper. The reaper is one of nine recorded delete paths. Enumerate all nine, state for each whether it is automatic or operator-invoked, and apply the protections to every automatic one. A guard on the single function that caused today's loss leaves eight doors open.

Measure the exposure before closing the path. Closing a fail-open guard converts whatever was passing because it was open into failures, all at once. Flip it in a scratch copy and count first; the number belongs in the brief rather than being discovered by the worker. Expect the exposed failures to be legitimate.

§3a — The guard is the primary filter where it should be the backstop, and it has stopped the pipeline

Observed in the wild 2026-09-09, by a node doing unrelated work: a broad sn run reached global maintenance and exited 1 on structural derived-parent cleanup, refusing over four protected identities — electron_power_density_due_to_collisions, ion_charge_state_power, ion_power_density and pressure_at_pedestal_top. The guard did exactly what §3 specified. The consequence is that global maintenance now fails every time it runs, because those identities carry recorded spend, are therefore permanently protected, and keep failing placeholder admission — so they sit permanently in the candidate set.

The defect is placement, not policy. refuse_protected_automatic_deletion is called at graph_ops.py:3672, after the candidate set is assembled, and its docstring is accurate: “Refuse a whole automatic batch when any identity is protected.” Whole-batch refusal is right — §3 ruled partial execution unacceptable and that ruling stands. But refusing at deletion time makes the guard the primary filter, so a routine “nothing here to reap” is raised as a fatal error and takes every other legitimate maintenance task down with it.

Exclude protected identities from candidate selection; keep the refusal as the backstop. A pass that never proposes to delete a protected identity has nothing to refuse, and the guard then fires only on a genuine surprise — which is what a backstop is for. This weakens nothing: the selector gains an exclusion, the refusal keeps its whole-batch semantics, and the ceiling is untouched. Note the distinction from §3's partial-execution ban, because the two are easy to conflate: not selecting a protected row is not the same as executing part of a batch that contains one.

The scope is far wider than global maintenance, and this section under-stated it when first written. Measured 2026-09-09 by the pedestal-family restore, which the defect blocked outright: write_standard_names hands the relationship-side skeleton_candidate_ids to refuse_protected_automatic_deletion before its deletion query applies the positive placeholder predicates. So the guard is evaluated against the broad relationship candidate set rather than against the query-proven deletable skeleton set, and ordinary composition of any paid family identity raises ProtectedDeletionError before a single StandardName can commit. The exact 21-source gap-only run produced 9 pool errors, 0 names composed, 0 of 19 children, and 0 of 21 producer edges — at $0.000000, so the failure was free but total.

The guard built to protect the restore is what prevents the restore. That is not an argument for weakening it; it is the strongest available argument for moving the evaluation to where the deletable set is actually known. A guard asked whether a relationship candidate is protected can only answer yes, because every paid identity is; the question it was designed to answer is whether a placeholder proposed for deletion is protected, and that set is not known until the positive predicates run.

Why no gate caught it. §3 was verified against tests/standard_names, and the default markers exclude graph, so live composition was never exercised. The repository's own guidance names this exactly — the default markers exclude graph, so Cypher you edit is never executed — and it bit the guard itself. A protection whose whole subject is a live graph write cannot be proven by a suite that never reaches the graph.

Done when the exact 21-source gap-only pedestal run composes without raising, a test fails if the positive-predicate ordering is reverted so the guard again sees the broad candidate set, a second test still proves refuse_protected_automatic_deletion raises when a protected identity is handed to the deleter directly, and a broad sn run completes global maintenance without refusing over a protected identity. --skip-global-maintenance conceals the maintenance half and does nothing for the composition half, so it is not a workaround for this — it is what hid the wider scope until a restore tried to run.

§4a — The pipeline entry point, and the flag that would silently do nothing

Read from imas-codex sn run --help on 2026-09-08 and not yet measured; treat the semantics as documented rather than proven, and spend one free --dry-run before the first paid pass.

Scope is --focus <dd/path>, repeatable, with the same paths accepted positionally; --focus <file>.yaml takes a manifest of sources. For anything release-bound use --batch <name> instead: it resolves standard_names/manifests/<name>.yaml and behaves exactly like --focus on that file, but the same token also drives sn release --batch, so one batch identity carries through mop-up, release and approval. An ad-hoc --focus does not.

The default is gap-only, and that is precisely what a restore wants. A path that already carries a live accepted name is left untouched; only paths lacking one are seeded and processed. A deleted name has no live accepted name, so the default seeds exactly the deleted set and leaves every healthy sibling alone. It follows that --reseed and --force must not be passed: --reseed re-stages paths that do carry a live accepted name and resets them to pending, and --force re-generates names for already-named sources. Either would churn names that are fine, which is the opposite of a restore.

--name is the intuitive flag and it cannot do this job. It scopes the worker pools to existing StandardName identities and never seeds DD sources. The identities here are deleted, so there is nothing for it to scope to: the command either fails preflight or no-ops, and both outcomes look exactly like a working command that did nothing. Establish which, with a free --dry-run, before any route depends on the seeding path.

-c/--cost-limit takes the cap in USD, so the authorised ceiling is enforced by the command rather than by discipline. -t/--time is minutes, not threads. --dry-run previews extraction with no LLM calls and costs nothing.

§2b — The ledger only augments: once paid, an item stays paid

Lead ruling 2026-09-09: LLMCost always augments and is never wiped. Once paid, an item stays paid. The cost ledger is not derived state and not a cache — it is the record of money spent, and the only instrument that can answer whether an identity was invested in. Nothing may remove a cost row.

This closes the hole the delete-path inventory found, and it is the hole that mattered most because it sits under §2 rather than beside it. §2 makes recorded spend the boundary an automatic pass may not cross. But two operator-invoked paths delete the ledger outright — graph_ops.py:9021 on an unscoped clear, and graph_ops.py:9133 — so a protection whose evidence an authorised path can erase is one deletion away from being unenforceable. An identity protected at 11:00 becomes reapable at 11:01, and nothing about the identity changed. The guard would keep reporting a safety it no longer provides, which is the exact failure shape this plan was opened to remove.

Both statements go. A clear that must reset pipeline state has no standing to forget what was paid: the two axes are independent, and conflating them is what let a convenience operation destroy an audit record. Where an operator genuinely needs the ledger to stop describing live identities, the answer is that cost rows outlive the identities they name — an orphaned cost row is a true statement about money that was spent, and deleting it makes the total lie.

Done when no code path deletes a LLMCost node, a test fails if either statement is reinstated, an unscoped clear leaves the cost-row count unchanged while still resetting whatever it is entitled to reset, and the recorded all-time spend total is the same before and after that clear. The negative half is the one to gate on: count the rows after, not the absence of an error.

§4b — A retargeted producer means the quantity has a home; define the home

Lead ruling 2026-09-09, and it is a better answer than the three dispositions put to it. plasma_beta is not restored and is not merely left absent: it becomes beta, the global beta, defined with 1/β = 1/βt + 1/βp.

The relation checks out and its derivation is worth recording, because it is what makes the name a quantity rather than a category. With a common volume-averaged perpendicular pressure ⟨p⟩ and the total field adding in quadrature, B2 = Bt2 + Bp2:

β = 2μ0⟨p⟩/B2,   βt = 2μ0⟨p⟩/Bt2,   βp = 2μ0⟨p⟩/Bp2
⇒ 1/β = (Bt2 + Bp2)/(2μ0⟨p⟩) = 1/βt + 1/βp

So the reciprocal sum is not a convention — it follows from component fields adding in quadrature over a shared pressure, which is why the total beta is the harmonic combination of its component betas rather than their sum.

The graph is already shaped for this, and its current state is the defect. beta is live at name_stage=accepted but docs_stage=drafted, unit='1', carrying exactly the three children toroidal_beta, poloidal_beta and normalized_toroidal_beta — and no producer at all, which is the one TRUTHFUL — producer gap row the label census found. Its description today reads “Dimensionless equilibrium pressure-to-magnetic-pressure measure, with concrete forms using toroidal or poloidal field scales and optional normalization” — a description of a family, not of a quantity. That is precisely what the ruling corrects.

The general rule, since this recurs wherever a source moved after a deletion. An archived producer that now truthfully targets a live identity is evidence the quantity survived, never an edge to reinstate — reinstating it would create dual source authority against the live scalar. But the disposition is not simply to walk away: where the deleted name was a vaguer spelling of a concept that has a proper home, resolve it by defining that home correctly. The deleted row's ratification is then satisfied by a name that says what the quantity is, which is a better outcome than either restoring a duplicate or recording a loss. Here all seven beta_tor sources agree on toroidal_beta, scalar and edge alike, so plasma_beta's producer was never coming back — and its concept was the global beta all along.

Two mechanism constraints, both easy to get wrong. The change goes through sn edit on the docs axis and nowhere else — hand-editing graph text bypasses the grammar validation, review quorum and scoring that make the catalog trustworthy. And because beta is a parent, sn edit defaults to --scope subtree, which would cascade into all three children; this edit must pass --scope self explicitly. Separately, beta's missing canonical derived:beta producer remains its own provenance repair.

§7a — Lead ruling: the sweep is a retirement, not a reclassification

Lead ruling 2026-09-08. Asked whether the proper sweep meant writing a truthful value onto every one of the 412 rows or resolving the field at its root, the lead chose retirement. The end state is therefore structural rather than corrective: the 334 stop being mislabelled because the label ceases to exist, and being derived becomes a fact read off an edge that is already present and already truer than the scalar.

This executes origin-axis-disposition = retire-origin-entirely, the lead ruling of 2026-09-04 locked on sn-lifecycle-integrity, whose evidence is stronger than this section's own measurement: exactly one writer of StandardName.origin exists in the codebase, graph_ops.py:4002, and it writes derived; nothing writes catalog_edit or pipeline at all, so 3,384 markers are residue from a path that no longer exists; and the surviving derived duplicates the StandardNameSource source-type binding while disagreeing with it 341 times — 18 marked without a derived source, 323 carrying one and unmarked.

Four stages, in order, and the order is the safety property:

  1. Audit — the reader inventory and the disagreement set. In flight.
  2. Repoint every reader at the source binding.
  3. Resolve the protection gate under its own locked decision, protection-trigger = approved-states-only.
  4. Drop the field.

No regeneration is implied and no LLM spend is incurred: filter_protected only strips fields from writes the pipeline is already making, and pool eligibility keys on name_stage and docs_stage, never on origin.

What this does to the restored-origin decision, and why it is not reopened. restored-origin-value writes origin='pipeline' onto restored names — into the field stage 4 removes. That is residue, not a contradiction, and it is deliberately left alone: the §3 guard is what keeps a restored identity safe, not its origin value, so the write carries no load and costs nothing, and stage 4 removes it with everything else. The binding constraint is narrower and it is absolute: nothing in the restore may be made to depend on the value.

The risk that dictates the sequence. A reader missed at stage 2 does not fail — it decides differently, silently. That is why the audit comes first, and why its reader table must record, per site, what the branch does with the value rather than only that it reads it. Nine sites are known to read it and not one has been audited.

Is any non-zero recorded spend enough to make an identity undeletable, or should there be a floor?

Any non-zero recorded spend makes an identity undeletable by an automatic pass. No floor: the money is not the point, the spend record is evidence that the pipeline invested in this identity. The eighteen zero-spend rows in the incident set show the test already discriminates without a threshold.

How should the guard read spend — a set-based pre-check over LLMCost , or a materialised edge from cost to identity?

Materialise the cost-to-identity edge so the protection is cheap enough to apply at every delete site rather than only in the one pass that can afford a scan. standard_name_ids is an unindexed list property over roughly 37,000 rows: viable once per pass, not once per identity. It is also the honest shape, since this repository holds that an edge is the source of truth and a scalar derived from it, and a list of ids inside a cost row is the same mirrored-scalar pattern that produced the other defects on this surface.

Restore only the identities carrying spend, or every identity the cleanup removed?

Restore the identities carrying recorded spend, from the pass of 2026-09-08 first. The zero-spend rows are the ones the cleanup had the best claim to remove and restoring them would reinstate scaffolding the admission gate legitimately rejects.
